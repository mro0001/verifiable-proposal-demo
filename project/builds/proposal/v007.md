# An AI-Enhanced Workflow for Verifiable Social Science

AI can enhance the productivity of scholars, the quality of scholarship, and the rigor of science by offloading laborious tasks and lowering technical and knowledge barriers. However, the probabilistic nature of Large Language Models means that AI produces output that is often 1) plausible but misleading and 2) inconsistent across repeated uses. The plausibility and consistency of AI outputs are not adequately addressed by the existing transparency and reproducibility practices in social science. For social science researchers to trust a scholarly contribution produced by AI, scholars require verification that it is correct and that it was produced by the methods the author intended. We propose a model- and harness-agnostic workflow integrating verification, observability, and traceability into reproducible social science research. Implementing the proposed workflow allows AI contributions to become checkable and their production auditable, ultimately supporting ambitious AI-accelerated research and reusable workflow infrastructure that supports cumulative work.

AI-generated content is entering scientific manuscripts at a staggering pace [@liang2025; @kusumegi2025], and standards for using AI in research have lagged. AI can lower the cost of writing, coding, literature search, and other research tasks, allowing scholars to undertake work that would otherwise require more time, technical expertise, or cross-disciplinary collaborators [@doshi2024; @lee2024; @boiko2023]. These capabilities lower the cost of exploring risky ideas and help scholars work across fields and languages [@zheng2026]. But the power of AI to accelerate research has numerous negative consequences beyond its ethical and environmental concerns [@resnik2025; @luccioni2024]. The rising volume of manuscripts is straining the peer review process [@gartenberg2026] and AI's polished output can mask underlying flaws [@dellacqua2026], increasing the rate of low-quality submissions [@kusumegi2025; @suchak2025]. Scholars want clear guidance on acceptable use [@schulz2026; @oxford2026; @wiley2025], but journal policies rarely go beyond disclosure [@he2026; @ans2026]. The gap is a lack of systematic procedures for establishing what AI contributed, whether those contributions meet appropriate standards, and documentation of how the contributions were produced.

AI presents two challenges to rigorous science that current transparency and reproducibility practices do not address: the plausibility problem and inconsistency across identical uses. The plausibility problem occurs when AI produces plausible, yet incorrect outputs or surreptitiously generates the output using a process the scholar did not intend. Plausibility failures appear in hallucinated citations [@walters2023], misattributed claims [@linardon2026], and analyses that reach a defensible answer while skipping required steps [@kapoor2026]. Inconsistency occurs when generative AI models emit inconsistent output across runs with identical inputs. Inconsistency occurs because models sample their output from probabilities and GPU arithmetic shifts with hardware and server load [@yuan2025].

## Our approach and contribution

Our core contribution is a model- and harness-agnostic workflow that integrates verification, observability, and traceability into reproducible research. The workflow treats AI contributions as objects whose production, compliance with specified requirements, and incorporation into research products should be inspectable and documented. We use procedural graphs [@lu2026] stored in a queryable knowledge base to route AI contributions through verification procedures [JCGM -@jcgm2012], which is supported by auditable records of AI sessions and tool use. This workflow aims to support scholarly accountability through documented evidence rather than disclosure alone [@he2026; @ans2026].

The workflow builds on a plain-text reproducible research pipeline, which assembles manuscripts from text, references, and analysis code through a build script [@healy2020]. A build shows how a research product, such as a manuscript, website, research compendium, or presentation, was assembled, but not whether AI followed the intended procedure or whether a source supports a claim. Similarly, transcripts and agent logs record AI activity but do not establish that its contributions meet specified requirements. The workflow links these functions by implementing explicit human verification of AI contributions, recording AI session transcripts and tool use, and carrying accepted contributions into a reproducible pipeline. Verification, observability, and auditability enable more trustworthy AI use by making contributions checkable, their production visible, and their origins documented.

Verification is the "provision of objective evidence that a given item fulfils specified requirements" [JCGM -@jcgm2012, 2.44 and Note 2] and is implemented in a verification layer. Procedural graphs document steps for implementing a verification procedure while a code script is triggered to build the verification report. Process verification procedures check whether AI performed its assigned task (for example, reading a paper before summarizing it); output verification procedures compare the factuality and accuracy of AI contributions (for example, checking to make sure a reference is not hallucinated). Passing a check establishes compliance with requirements as judged by the scholar.

Observability concerns real-time monitoring of the process behind an AI output in an observability layer, which is hidden from anyone who sees only the output [@astrom2008]. In real time, the scholar may observe which files the harness reads, which tools it calls, and which knowledge base entries it retrieves, so a harness asked to apply a verification procedure can be seen retrieving it in addition to the LLM reasoning behind these choices. Observability is less formal than verification and comes earlier, allowing quick correction.

Audibility is a documented chain from each element of a research product to the inputs, sources, scripts, and sessions that produced it [JCGM -@jcgm2012, 2.41] referred to as an audit layer in this workflow. Because repeating an AI interaction may not reproduce the same output, maintaining session records and logs is essential for auditing and downstream verification [Hosseini, Resnik, and Holmes -@hosseini2023]. The audit layer records, stores, and tags every AI session transcript and tool call, so that any AI contribution can be reviewed either in real-time as part of the observability layer or more formally as part of the verification layer.

The proposed workflow extends reproducible research infrastructure beyond assembling reproducible manuscripts. It provides a way to make the procedures governing AI-assisted research explicit, executable, and open to systematic scrutiny. Its reusable procedural specifications could support the development and critique of research standards across projects, while its model- and harness-agnostic design aims to accommodate diverse researcher preferences and software ecosystems. The resulting infrastructure would give researchers a practical foundation for more systematic AI-assisted inquiry and enable journals, research teams, and professional organizations to consider evidence-based accountability requirements.

## System overview

The workflow operationalizes verification, observability, and traceability by processing AI contributions through a common sequence of identification, verification, review, and incorporation into a reproducible research product. It organizes AI contributions into four streams (Table 1 and Figure 1): prose, references, data and analysis code, and build files. These four streams broadly represent the types of AI outputs that show a direct impact on a research product and require different verification standards. The workflow identifies contributions to a research product and produces a verification report for a scholar's review and approval. Creating a record indicating exactly where and how a research product received AI contributions.

  Data and analysis code   R, Python, Quarto   Code

  Build                    Pandoc, Make        Build integrity

The architecture has four components that enable cross project work (Figure 2): AI harness, databases, reproducible pipeline, and external software. The AI harness is the software that connects to an AI model and connects it to tools and files [@zhang2026]. It is the scholar's point of contact with AI and AI's point of contact with the rest of the workflow, and events in the harness, such as an AI edit or the end of a turn, start the verification procedures. The workflow's databases hold the scholar's working knowledge base, procedural graphs, session transcripts and action logs and specially designed stores for scholarly manuscripts and research data. The reproducible pipeline assembles research products from text, references, data, and code. External software includes the researcher's editor, statistical software, citation manager, version control, and build tools such as Pandoc, Make, or Quarto, which is connected to the workflow through wrappers and Model Context Protocol servers. This architecture is designed to separate the research procedures and evidence requirements from any particular AI model or harness. Its practical agnosticism depends on the availability of interfaces that expose the events and information required by the workflow; we will evaluate this portability across multiple harnesses, models, and software integrations.

The workflow also supports reusable, project-specific features and AI-assisted project organization. Pipeline setup can help researchers establish a reproducible project structure and connect their existing software environment. A project organizer can assist with maintaining files, builds, and session records as work evolves, with changes intended to remain observable and reversible. These features apply the core workflow to a practical barrier to adoption: researchers often lack the time or technical expertise to establish and maintain reproducible infrastructure [@stodden2010] and work in disjointed and loosely organized ways, which leaves code and project files messy and often unable to run [Rule, Tabard, and Hollan -@rule2018; @eubank2016; @trisovic2022]. The procedural graph system also provides a potential mechanism for reusing institution- and researcher-specific operational procedures. Further, the procedural graph system enables scholars to implement reusable procedures for the operational tasks around research that are unique to a scholar's institution and preferences, such as connecting to a university high-performance computing cluster or adding a project to version control.

## Current Status and Development Plan

The proposal builds on a working prototype the details of which can be found in the technical appendix. We have built and tested the reproducible pipeline, the databases, the verification layer, the observability layer, and the two features, pipeline setup and project organizer above and the prototype can be accessed <https://github.com/mro0001/verifiable-social-science-workflow> while a demo of the verification reports run for this proposal can be found at <https://github.com/mro0001/verifiable-proposal-demo>.

In Stage 2 (January to May 2027) we will complete the verification layer, so that verification procedures, wrappers, MCP servers, and operational tasks are all written as procedural graphs. We will extend the observability layer so that software outside the workflow feeds the same live view of the internal workflow components. Finally, we will extend the workflow to additional harnesses and models.

## Key risks

Two key risks could limit the development, timely execution, and impact of the proposed workflow. First, the prototype is complex and the development of the system in stage two will need to focus on limiting AI content bloat and making the information scholars are most interested in reviewing, easy to find and understand. Second, the verification process may become verification theater in practice. Until standards are developed and adopted by the scientific community, verification will be practiced intermittently and reported seldomly.

## Implications

If the workflow is successfully built and disseminated, social scientists would gain both conceptual guidance and practical implementation support for AI-assisted research. In addition, researchers would have a foundation for developing their personal AI-assisted workflow enabling them to reuse procedures and audit records and standardize internal verification and observability of AI use in scientific teams. Finally, journals, conferences, and professional organizations could move from disclosure statements to reporting requirements that ask for evidence, such as a verification report and the session record behind each AI contribution.
